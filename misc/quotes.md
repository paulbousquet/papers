* For fixed impulse response functions, one can study dynamic responses of many variables
to an innovation in one variable. This approach is taken in “event studies,” for example,
about central banks’ “quantitative easings.” Because surprises can’t be systematically
chosen ex ante, fixed impulse response functions provide little help in designing improved
policies. A more ambitious application characterizes impulse response functions as functionals
of (parameterized) government policy rules for a manifold of equilibrium Markov models. [Sargent (2024)](http://www.tomsargent.com/research/Macro_after_Lucas.pdf)
  * Most policies will have general equilibrium effects that the average structural function
does not capture (the so-called Lucas critique). Barnichon and Mesters (2023) and McKay
and Wolf (2023) show that impulse response functions can be used as inputs into evaluating
counterfactuals and optimal policy rules in certain classes of linear structural macroeconomic
models, but their results heavily leverage the linearity assumption and the resulting certainty
equivalence. To our knowledge, it is an open question how to tackle the Lucas critique in
nonlinear settings without imposing a complete structural model. [Rejoiner](https://www.mikkelpm.com/files/nonlinear_causal_rejoinder.pdf) to Kolesár and Plagborg-Møller (2025)

* "Rational expectations econometrics, as Lars and I practiced it, treated the historical sample period asymmetrically. Private agents optimized. The government followed an arbitrary stochastic process. After estimating the preferences and technologies of private agents, we let a purposeful government choose a better rule for the future. I read Chris as objecting to that asymmetry. If governments had also been purposeful during the sample, the same game would continue to be played after it, and the same stochastic process would prevail. . He thought of a government as a sequence of administrations, none able to bind its successors, not as one infinitely lived planner choosing a regime once and for all. “Permanent shifts in policy regime are by definition rare events,” he wrote. “If they occurred often, they would not be permanent.” In the language I used above, Chris thought that predictability within the historical stochastic process gave policymakers the advice they actually seek. I wanted parameters invariant across changes in policy rules." Tom Sargent's Nobel biography
  * (A VAR fit to history would then deliver good forecasts and good projections conditioned on paths for policy variables. Chris put it this way: “Accurate projections can be made from reduced form models fit to history because it is not proposed to change the policy rule, only to implement effectively the existing rule.”)
